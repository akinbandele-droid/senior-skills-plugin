# Composition & Layout

How elements are arranged on the page. Composition is where a design succeeds or fails —
it directs attention, creates order, and makes the message effortless to receive.

## Visual hierarchy (the most important thing)
- Decide the **reading order**: what the viewer sees 1st, 2nd, 3rd. Build it with:
  **size** (bigger = more important), **weight/boldness**, **color & contrast**, **position**
  (top/left and optical center draw first), and **white space** (isolation = emphasis).
- **One clear focal point.** Establish a dominant element; everything else is subordinate. If
  everything competes, the eye bounces and the message is lost.
- Make hierarchy survive the **squint test**: blur your eyes — the most important thing should
  still stand out.

## Grids & alignment
- A **grid** (columns, margins, gutters, a baseline) gives structure, consistency, and speed.
  Common: multi-column grids, modular grids, the rule-of-thirds for focal placement.
- **Align everything to something.** Strong edges (left-align text, shared baselines, consistent
  margins) read as intentional and calm; stray misalignments read as sloppy even if the viewer
  can't name why. Pick alignments deliberately; avoid "almost aligned."
- Break the grid *intentionally* for emphasis once the grid is established — a deliberate break
  draws the eye; an accidental one just looks off.

## White space (negative space)
- Space is an active design element, not waste. It creates focus, groups and separates, conveys
  premium/calm, and improves comprehension. Crowding everything to "use the space" destroys hierarchy.
- Give the focal point and key type room to breathe. Generous margins and spacing almost always
  look more professional than dense ones.

## Balance & movement
- **Balance** the visual weight: **symmetry** (formal, stable, classic) vs. **asymmetry**
  (dynamic, modern — balanced by contrast, e.g. one large element against several small).
  Avoid accidental lopsidedness.
- Create a **path** for the eye (a Z- or F-pattern for text-heavy layouts; a deliberate flow from
  focal point through supporting elements to the call-to-action/resolution).
- **Contrast** creates interest and hierarchy (big/small, light/dark, thick/thin, busy/calm).
  Lack of contrast reads as monotonous; use it purposefully.

## Gestalt principles (how we group what we see)
- **Proximity** — things near each other are read as related (the strongest grouping tool: space
  groups better than lines/boxes). **Similarity** — things that look alike are read as a set.
- **Alignment/continuity** — the eye follows lines and aligned edges. **Closure** — we complete
  implied shapes. **Figure/ground** — clear separation of subject from background.
- Use these to group related content and separate unrelated content *without* heavy dividers.

## Working process
- Thumbnail small and fast to test many arrangements before refining one. Design with real content
  and at the final size/aspect ratio. Check it at viewing distance.

## Common pitfalls
- No clear focal point / flat hierarchy — everything the same size and weight.
- Poor or "almost" alignment; ignoring a grid. Cramped layouts with no white space.
- Centering everything by default (weak, static); accidental imbalance.
- Using boxes and lines to group what proximity/space would group more cleanly.
- Designing with placeholder text that hides real length and breaks later.
